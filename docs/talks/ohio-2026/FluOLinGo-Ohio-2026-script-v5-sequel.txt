
20. A recipe for your language

So here's the recipe, for anyone teaching a less commonly taught language or an Indigenous language.

One. Start with your textbook. List what it says learners should be able to do, and the grammar, vocabulary and pronunciation it provides. If it foregrounds only one side, work out the other: from purpose to resources, or from structure to function.

Two. Separate three kinds of step: linguistic goals, functional goals and tasks. On my first map, negation, inviting someone and writing an email all looked the same. Make sure your learners can tell a building block from the thing it builds.

Three. Connect them. For each functional goal, note which linguistic goals it draws on. That network tells you what a learner needs before each task, and where the same resource comes back.

Four. Choose the activity for the goal, not for the platform. Ask what would show that the goal has been reached. For a Mandarin tone, hearing it and saying it are not the same evidence, so they may need different activities.

Five. Keep teaching and assessment within what your textbook actually teaches. Learners can meet new language along the way, but they should never need it to get the answer right.

Six. Write down your decisions, and check the actual screens. Here's a real example. One of my lesson pages had a single exercise with three levels of support. An AI helper split it into several separate activities. Each looked fine on its own, but the progression, from recognising to producing, was gone, and only a teacher would notice. So I now write my teaching decisions in plain language, make sure the AI always has them, and look at the screen myself rather than trusting a description of it. Whether you're working with AI or with a person, the teacher remains the author.

21. Where it stands

So where are things now? The French version is live, and planning the Chinese version has already changed it: the fifty identical stops have become linguistic goals, functional goals and tasks.

As for the Chinese course, I am still working out its design, including its units, goals and activities. The build starts after this conference, so I can't show you a Chinese screen today. If you are interested, let me know, and I will share it when it is done.

Let's go back to my question, then. Can the organisation of language learning be separated from the language being learned? From what I've designed so far, much of it can. The goals, the links between them and the learning cycle carry over to Mandarin. The goal inventory, the progression and several activities do not, and tones and characters force me to rethink what counts as achieving a goal. Whether the result works as well for Chinese learners is the next question, and only the build and testing will answer it.
