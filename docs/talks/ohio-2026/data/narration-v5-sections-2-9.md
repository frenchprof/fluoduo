2. Introduction

Today I want to talk about FluOLinGo. That’s capital F, O, L and G, and it stands for Fluency On Linguistic Goals.

It’s a platform for beginners learning French, and I built it myself, with no coding background and no development team.

3. What I mean by goals

As the name suggests, the whole thing is organised around goals.

I call them goals, but they’re really what teachers know as learning objectives. The communicative ones are much like what ACTFL calls can-do statements.

Instead of organising learning mainly around chapters or exercises, I organise it around what learners need to know and what they should be able to do.

4. Two kinds of goals

In the 1970s, the applied linguist David Wilkins argued that language teaching should consider not just grammar, but also the meanings and communicative functions learners need to express.

FluOLinGo brings these two concerns together.

I distinguish between two kinds of goals.

Linguistic goals focus on the language resources learners need, such as vocabulary, grammar and pronunciation.

Communicative goals focus on what learners can accomplish with those resources, like saying what they like or inviting a friend out.

For the linguistic goals, I draw on distinctions found in frameworks such as the CEFR.

I use five categories: phonological, lexical, morphological, syntactic and orthographic. In everyday terms, these concern sounds, vocabulary, word forms, sentence structure and writing conventions.

Morphology and syntax are both parts of grammar, but I separate them because they present different learning challenges.

These five categories give me a practical way to organise the language resources beginners need. Other aspects, such as organising ideas coherently or choosing language appropriate to a social situation, are addressed through communicative goals and tasks.

5. The question and the plan

Wilkins developed his approach in the context of European language teaching.

But could the same principles work for Chinese, a language so different from French?

What could stay the same, and what would have to change?

That’s the question I want to explore today.

I’ll start with the French course, then look at what makes Chinese different. I’ll show you how the same approach can be adapted, using examples from both textbooks, and finish with some steps you can try with your own language.

6. From fifty identical stops to three kinds of step

This is where FluOLinGo began. It teaches beginner French using our course book.

In the first version, I divided the course into fifty small goals across five units and laid them out like Candy Crush stops on a map.

Every exercise belonged to a goal. In other words, I started with the goal, then built the exercises around it.

But I ran into a problem. Some of the stops looked exactly the same on the map, even though they represented very different things.

Number twenty-eight, negation, is linguistic.

Number twenty-nine, inviting someone and turning down an invitation, is communicative.

Number thirty, writing an email, is a larger task.

Negation is one linguistic building block. Inviting someone is something you do with language. Writing an email to arrange an outing is a larger piece of work that can bring several linguistic and communicative goals together.

So a learner moving along the map could not tell a small step from a big one, or see how the different steps were connected.

At first, I didn’t notice this, because I was simply following the French book, which already organises its content into situations and tasks.

But when I started planning the Chinese version, things changed. The Chinese book is organised around dialogues, without clearly labelled situations or tasks.

That forced me to look more closely at what each stop actually represented.

And I realised that my map was mixing three different things: linguistic goals, communicative goals and tasks.

On the right is the revised structure.

Each unit now has two kinds of goal, linguistic and communicative, followed by one cumulative task where learners bring everything together.

And you’ll see that same structure in both languages.

7. Connecting linguistic and communicative goals

Let me explain how the two kinds of goal are organised.

Each linguistic goal has its own stop on the map, with a dedicated lesson page.

Communicative goals also have their own stops, but instead of a lesson page, each leads to four activities: listening, reading, speaking and writing.

Now, the French textbook, L’Atelier, organises its content into broad situations.

For example, “talking about your tastes” is one such situation. It includes three communicative goals: saying what you like, saying what you do and explaining why.

In FluOLinGo, I separate these into three individual stops on the map.

Alongside them are the linguistic goals: the vocabulary, grammar, sounds and writing conventions learners work on throughout the unit.

But how do the two kinds fit together?

They meet in a grid.

Linguistic goals go down the side, and communicative goals go across the top.

Here’s part of Unit 2, for example.

“To like the” helps you say what you like, and also say why. Negation, for its part, turns up in several communicative goals at once.

In other words, one linguistic goal can support several communicative goals, and one communicative goal can draw on several linguistic goals.

The grid shows which linguistic resources are relevant to each communicative goal, and where they can be practised and reinforced.

This reflects the distinction Wilkins made between synthetic syllabuses, which build language up from separate elements, and analytic syllabuses, which organise learning around meaningful language use.

Janice Yalden later proposed a proportional syllabus, combining structural and communicative elements in changing proportions as learners progress.

As a result of this restructuring, the revised FluOLinGo French course now has around sixty linguistic goals, while the book’s twelve broad situations become about three dozen distinct communicative goals.

Finally, every unit finishes with one cumulative task that brings together its linguistic and communicative goals. The French book calls it the Mission.

So that’s the overall structure. Now let me show you what happens at each kind of stop.

8. One lesson page per linguistic goal

Let’s start with the linguistic goals.

Each one gets its own lesson page, although a single goal can sometimes bring together closely related elements.

In Unit 0, for example, the days of the week come together with the moments of the day: morning and evening, for instance.

That’s because beginners need both from the start, and they naturally combine them in expressions like “Monday morning”.

Each lesson page has six tabs.

Learners start with the course path, then move through explanations, forms and exercises, with bonus material and a word list at the end.

Let’s look more closely at the exercise tab.

Here is an example from another lesson page, for “to like”.

It uses a single sentence generator with three levels.

Each sentence starts from pictures: for example, “we”, a broken heart and a piano.

At one star, the verb is given, and learners choose the correct form from a list.

At two stars, they choose both the verb form and the article.

At three stars, they type the whole sentence themselves.

And in the Bonus, they’re given the English and have to produce the French without any help.

So the same sentence generator takes learners from recognition to independent production, with progressively less support.

One exercise, three levels, all on the same page.

9. One communicative goal, four language skills

Now let’s look at a communicative goal.

We’ll stay in French Unit 2 and take “saying what you like” as an example. You can see the French title here.

Learners first work on the relevant linguistic goals, using the lesson pages and exercises we’ve just seen.

They then move to the communicative goal, where they practise through four activities.

This introduces another dimension: the four language skills.

Linguistic goals concern the resources learners need. Communicative goals concern what they want to accomplish. The four skills provide different ways of working towards those goals.

In ÉcouTexte, learners listen to spoken examples of the language in use.

In G-Compris, which means “I understood,” they read a short everyday document, like the message on the right, and answer questions designed to check their understanding of the language taught.

In WorDrill, they repeat words and sentences aloud and receive immediate feedback on how closely their speech matches the model.

And in ComposeIt, they write a few lines of their own and receive feedback from an AI chatbot.

So these four activities approach the same communicative goal through listening, reading, speaking and writing.

Finally, at the end of the unit, there’s the Mission: planning a weekend outing together.

Unlike the four activities, the Mission draws on all the communicative goals in the unit, together with the linguistic resources that support them.

And throughout, learners can follow their progress on the map, moving from individual goals towards the final task.
