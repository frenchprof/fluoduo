# Opening

## 1. Self-intro

Before we start, a quick word. The person you see in this video is an AI clone of me. The ideas and the words, though, are all mine.

I’m Daniel Chan. I teach at the Centre for Language Studies at NUS, and I’ve been teaching French for over twenty-five years.

## 2. Introduction

Today I want to talk about FluOLinGo. That’s capital F, O, L and G, and it stands for Fluency On Linguistic Goals.

It’s a platform for beginners learning French, and I built it myself, with no coding background and no development team.

## 3. What I mean by goals

As the name suggests, the whole thing is organised around goals.

I call them goals, but they’re really what teachers know as learning objectives. And the communicative goals are much like what ACTFL calls can-do statements.

## 4. Two kinds of goals

In the 1970s, the applied linguist David Wilkins argued that a syllabus should cover the meanings and functions learners need to express, alongside the grammar.

FluOLinGo uses both.

Linguistic goals are about the language itself. Communicative goals are about what you do with it, like saying what you like or inviting a friend out.

Later frameworks, like the CEFR, went further and distinguished between sounds, vocabulary, grammar and writing.

In this talk, I split grammar into two: word forms, like verb endings, and sentence structure, like word order.

So I end up with five types of linguistic goal: sounds, vocabulary, word forms, sentence structure and script.

## 5. The question and the plan

Wilkins was working on a framework for European languages. But could the same principles work for a language as different as Chinese?

That’s the question I want to look at today.

Can a platform built for French work for Chinese, a language that’s so different? And if so, what can stay the same, and what has to change?

I’ll start with the French course, then look at what makes Chinese different. I’ll show you how the same approach can be adapted, using examples from both textbooks, and finish with some steps you can try with your own language.

# Part 1. The French course

## 6. From fifty goals that all looked alike to three kinds of step

This is where FluOLinGo began.

It teaches beginner French using our course book, L’Atelier.

In the first version, I cut the course into fifty small goals across five units and laid them out on a map.

That’s the left side of the slide: fifty numbered goals in a row.

Every exercise belonged to a goal. In other words, I started with the goal, then built the exercises around it.

But look at three of those goals.

Number twenty-four, “to do some of an activity,” is linguistic.

Number twenty-nine, inviting someone and turning down an invitation, is communicative.

Number thirty, writing an email, is a whole task.

On the map, they all looked the same.

It was planning the Chinese version that made me notice this.

On the right is the revised structure. Each unit now has three kinds of step: linguistic goals, the situations that use them, and one task at the end.

Only the first two are goals.

And you’ll see that same structure in both languages.

## 7. One lesson page per linguistic goal

This is the core of it: the lesson page.

I designed it myself, as a teacher, before any of the tech came in.

Every linguistic goal gets its own page. If two goals are easier to learn side by side, they share a page.

On my original page, “to like the” sits next to “to do some.”

Look at these two sentences: “I like yoga” and “I do yoga,” both in French.

There are six tabs. Learners start with the course path, then move through explanations, forms and exercises, with bonus material and a word list at the end.

The exercise tab is a single sentence generator with three levels.

At one star, you pick the verb form: “I like,” spelled correctly. You can see the common misspelling next to it.

At two stars, you pick the verb and the article together.

At three stars, you type the whole sentence yourself.

Then the Bonus gives you the English and asks for the French, with no help at all.

What I learned here: one exercise, three levels. Keep it on one page, with one generator. That’s it.

## 8. Each unit: linguistic goals, situations, one task

Each unit combines linguistic goals and communicative goals, which L’Atelier calls situations.

Each linguistic goal gets a lesson page, while each situation represents something learners can do with the language.

Sometimes the book packs several things into one situation. When it does, I split them up, so each situation has one purpose.

The two kinds meet in a grid.

Linguistic goals go down the side, situations go across the top.

Here’s part of Unit 2.

“To like the” helps you say what you like, and also say why. Negation turns up in several situations at once.

One linguistic goal can serve lots of situations, and one situation can need lots of linguistic goals.

So with the grid, I can see at a glance what a learner needs before tackling each situation.

This actually brings together two approaches Wilkins talked about: building language up piece by piece, which is the synthetic approach, and starting with what learners need to do, which is the analytic approach.

Janice Yalden called something similar a proportional syllabus, bringing structure and communication together.

Every unit finishes with one task that uses all its situations. L’Atelier calls it the Mission.

In the new plan, the French course has thirty-seven situations and around sixty linguistic goals.

# Part 2. Two languages that are far apart

## 11. Where French and Chinese put the weight

Chinese has no articles and no verb conjugations.

What it does have is measure words, aspect markers and tones.

But you can still use the same five categories for both languages. In fact, I’d argue they work for any language.

What changes is how important each one is.

French and Mandarin overlap on vocabulary and sentence structure. Both use subject, verb, object.

Look at these two sentences: “I go skiing” and “I drink coffee.” They’re built almost the same way.

In French, word endings do a lot of the work.

In Chinese, sounds and vocabulary matter much more, and word order does a lot of the grammatical work.

So let’s go through the five categories.

Sounds carry a huge amount of weight.

Tones matter from the very first day. Get the tone wrong and you’ve said a different word.

Look at this example: one syllable in four tones. Mother, hemp, horse, scold. Four separate words.

In French, a pronunciation mistake usually gets forgiven. In Chinese, a tone mistake often won’t.

Vocabulary is another big challenge.

There are almost no cognates to help you.

And because of the characters, every word has to be learned twice: once by ear and once in writing.

Word forms, on the other hand, are much simpler.

Aspect particles do some of the work that tense endings do in French, without the conjugation tables.

But sentence structure matters enormously.

With little inflection, grammar relies on word order, topic and comment, and particles.

Look at the pair of sentences here. It’s a classic example.

Here’s something interesting.

Measure words give Chinese its own kind of agreement.

In French, every noun has a gender, and the article and adjective have to match it.

In Chinese, every noun has its own classifier.

Here you’ve got a fish, a book and a table, and each takes a different one.

You basically have to learn the classifier along with the noun.

So Chinese has plenty of grammar. It’s just moved out of the word endings and into the vocabulary and the sentence.

Writing is a different story altogether.

For Chinese, it’s a whole track of its own.

But some languages, including many Indigenous languages, are learned mostly by listening, so they have few script goals, or none.

# Part 3. Moving the template to Chinese

## 12. Frame, slots, settings

So how do I take what I’ve built for French and adapt it to Chinese?

I do it in three steps.

Step one: keep the frame.

Build the parts that work for any language just once.

There are also two important rules built into it: the catalogue and the spiral. I’ll come to those in a moment.

Step two: change the settings.

The exercises still work the same way, but learners make different choices.

Step three: fill the slots.

Use the new textbook to work out what learners need to be able to do.

That’s where the teacher does the real work.

Think of a loom.

The loom stays the same. That’s the frame.

The thread and pattern change with each language.

Let’s take the steps one at a time.

## 13. The frame: built once, filled twice

The frame is simply everything I can reuse, whatever language I’m teaching.

That includes the lesson pages, four types of exercise for sentences, words, set phrases and sounds, the different difficulty levels, bonus material, situation activities, unit tasks and the learning map.

For Chinese, the activities keep their structure, but the texts and words change.

There’s one more thing, and I actually only finalised it this week: the catalogue.

The rule is simple: never teach or test anything the book doesn’t cover.

I’ve designed the site to check that catalogue in four different places.

First, there’s a check that stops the site from building anything beyond the course limits.

Then there’s the path tab on each lesson, a page for each unit, and the search.

Type the French word for “midnight,” the one you see here, and the search tells you: Unit 2, Situation 2.

Type “half past,” and it tells you: that’s not in this course.

So I only need to build the system once, then put the French or Chinese content into it.

For French, the content comes from L’Atelier. For Chinese, it comes from New Practical Chinese Reader, Book 1.

But how do I decide what goes into the catalogue?

And how do I stop it from becoming too restrictive?

## 14. Every limit has a source

How do you build a catalogue?

From three sources: the textbook, the teacher’s guide and the publisher’s unit tests.

If a unit test asks for something, it counts as taught in that unit.

So whenever I decide that something belongs in a particular unit, I need evidence from one of those three sources.

I can’t just include something because I happen to know it.

Some examples.

“Quarter past” and “half past” aren’t in the French course.

The book tells the time as “nine fifteen” and “nine thirty.”

“Noon” and “midnight” are in, in Unit 2.

Whenever the book introduces a verb, learners practise it with all nine subject pronouns.

And in Chinese, Lesson 11 teaches the time with its own words for “a quarter,” “three quarters” and “half.”

So the Chinese course will teach them.

Now, I have to be honest here.

When I checked the first French version against the book, it went way past it.

Twelve colours in the first unit, when the book has two.

“Why” taught four units before the book gets to it.

And a pre-test that marked learners on “half past two,” which the book never teaches.

Every one of those choices seemed helpful at the time.

But learners were being marked on things the course hadn’t taught them.

That’s why I introduced the catalogue.

And I want the site to check those limits automatically whenever I update it.

## 15. Seen, Focus, Scored

Now, you might be thinking: so learners never come across anything new?

They do.

The French approach I follow is a spiral.

Learners meet things early, as whole phrases, and figure out how they work later.

So I’ve divided what learners encounter into three categories.

Seen means learners come across a phrase, but they don’t have to study it or get tested on it.

Focus means it’s time to learn it properly, with explanations and practice.

And Scored means learners can now be tested on it, but only once the course has taught it.

Think of a camera.

Something can be in the shot but blurry. That’s Seen.

Then you bring it into focus. That’s Focus.

And only then do you take the picture. That’s Scored.

For example, learners meet “I was born in” before studying the past tense, and “How are you?” before learning the verb “to go.”

I also allow a little unfamiliar language, roughly twenty percent.

People learn by struggling a bit.

If everything’s familiar, it’s too easy. If half of it is unknown, it’s too hard.

But learners should never need that unfamiliar language to get the answer right.

Look at the first sentence: “At weekends, I go … skiing.”

The answer is the word for “some,” so it doesn’t matter that “weekend” is new.

The second one says “It is two …”

There the answer would be “half past,” which is never taught.

So that one’s out.

## 16. The settings: the key choice moves

This is where French and Chinese start to look quite different.

I can keep the same exercise, but change what learners have to choose.

In French, at two stars, the key choice is the article.

You like yoga, so it takes “the.”

You do yoga, so it takes “some.”

Look at the two sentences here.

“The” versus “some” is exactly where beginners go wrong, so that’s the gap the learner has to fill.

Mandarin has no articles.

So what goes in the gap?

In this example, it’s the verb that goes with the object.

These all come from the first textbook.

Look at these three examples: play ball, see a film, drink coffee.

The learner matches each verb to its object.

Another good example is the measure word.

A piece of clothing takes the classifier you see here.

The typical beginner mistake is to use the general-purpose one.

So I can reuse the same exercise format, but change the answer depending on the language.

In French, “some” fits and “the” bounces off.

In Chinese, the classifier for clothing fits, and the general one bounces off.

Chinese also needs exercises for tones and characters, but these still fit into the same categories.

## 17. A word never taught, asked once

There’s another idea I can use in both languages, and I think it could work in others too.

I call it the transfer test.

At the end of an exercise run, I ask learners about a word they’ve never seen before.

In French, the course teaches the masculine and feminine words for “a Chinese person.”

Then it asks: what about Luxembourgish?

Nobody taught that one.

But learners know the rule: if the word ends in -s, you add -e.

So they can work out the answer by applying the rule. You can see it happening here.

That answer isn’t marked.

You can’t mark someone wrong on something you never told them.

The feedback simply gives them the rule.

Chinese works the same way, and the rule’s even simpler: country plus the word for “person” gives you the nationality.

China gives you “a Chinese person.”

Britain gives you “a British person.”

Then: Luxembourg?

Same pattern, and here’s the answer.

That helps me see whether learners have understood the rule or simply memorised the examples.

## 18. Filling the slots: the teacher writes them

Now the third step.

Here are the two courses side by side.

French follows L’Atelier: a welcome unit, then four themed units. You can see their French titles here.

Chinese follows New Practical Chinese Reader, Book 1, with its fourteen lessons regrouped into five units.

The French book names its situations, adds short workshops built around real documents, which it calls expression workshops, and ends each unit with a Mission.

For French, I mostly split up and sorted what was already there.

The Chinese book has none of that.

So I have to write the Chinese situations myself. Twenty-six of them in total.

Lessons 6 and 14 are review lessons, so I use those as the basis for the final tasks.

For the other units, I write the tasks too.

Let me show you what that looks like.

## 19. When the book gives you nothing: NPCR Lesson 3

New Practical Chinese Reader, Book 1, Lesson 3.

The title is “What country is she from?”

The book gives you a text, some notes and pronunciation practice.

No situations, no task, and this lesson doesn’t even have a grammar section.

So I start from what the text actually does.

Look at the first dialogue.

Someone asks who that is.

The answer: that’s our teacher.

Then: what country is she from?

She’s Chinese.

And then: all our teachers are Chinese.

From that short dialogue, I can identify two things learners need to do: identify someone and say where they’re from.

Next, the linguistic goals.

The verb “to be,” and how to make it negative.

The question word “who.”

The nationality pattern: country plus “person.”

The word “all.”

Learners have already seen it in Lesson 2, but they won’t study where it goes in a sentence until Lesson 7.

So for now, they just encounter it, without practising it separately.

And one sound: the negative word changes its tone before a fourth tone.

So “is” and “is not” are said with different tones. Notice the change here.

It’s the same grid as in French.

Only the content is new, and everything comes from the book.

I keep the same basic structure, but adapt the goals, exercises and situations to Chinese.

And the textbook determines what learners are expected to know.

That’s how the French template becomes a Chinese one.

## 20. Bonus material and teacher additions

There are two more categories I need, because textbooks don’t cover everything teachers want to teach.

The first is bonus material: things beyond the book that are still useful.

They’re labelled “bonus” on screen, never marked, and never needed to finish a step.

For example, the book has almost no school subjects, but my students want to introduce themselves.

“I study physics,” in French, as you can see here.

So school subjects stay in, as bonus.

The second category is the teacher addition.

That’s something I choose to teach, even though it isn’t in the textbook.

It’s taught and marked just like book content.

My first example is “pleased to meet you.”

The book never teaches it, but I teach it in the very first lesson.

The catalogue marks each item as either bonus or a teacher addition.

That way, the system knows I’ve included it deliberately, rather than by mistake.

# Part 4. Closing

## 21. AI builds fast. The teacher stays the author.

A word about how I built this, since the title talks about an AI platform with no development team.

I built FluOLinGo with AI coding tools.

They’re fast, and they made it possible for someone like me, who can’t code.

But they can get the teaching wrong.

Here’s a real example.

Remember the lesson page, with one exercise and three levels?

Earlier AI helpers broke that up into lots of separate activities.

Each looked fine by itself, but the teaching idea behind it was gone, and only a teacher would notice.

I’ve found two things that help prevent that.

First, I write down my teaching decisions in plain language and make sure the AI always has access to them.

Second, I look at the actual screen myself, because the AI’s description of it can be wrong.

Those written rules help stop the AI from making teaching decisions on its own.

And because every rule comes from a specific source, I can check whether the AI has followed it.

You can check my decisions the same way.

The teacher has to stay the author.

## 22. A recipe for your language

So here’s the recipe, for anyone teaching a less commonly taught language or an Indigenous language.

One. Start with your textbook. Identify what learners need to learn, and what they need to be able to do.

Two. Make a list of exactly what your textbook teaches. Keep teaching and assessment within those limits, while allowing learners to encounter some new language along the way.

Three. Give each linguistic goal its own lesson page, and create a few exercise formats you can reuse.

Four. Write your own situations where your book doesn’t give you any. No tool can do that part for you.

Five. One exercise, more levels. If something’s too easy or too hard, add a level and keep the same screen.

Six. Write down your decisions and check the actual screens. Whether you’re working with AI or with a person, the teacher remains the author.

French and Chinese are very different languages.

If the same approach works for both, that would be a good reason to try it with others.
