# FluOLinGo — Ohio 2026 · narration canvas (FINAL — English voice only)

Before we begin: The presenter you see in this video is an AI clone of me, but all the ideas and words it contains are entirely and genuinely mine.

I am Daniel Chan, a Senior Lecturer at the NUS Centre for Language Studies, and I have taught French as an Additional Language for more than twenty-five years.

My presentation is about FluOLinGo, spelled with a capital F, O, L, and G, and it stands for Fluency On Linguistic Goals. FluOLinGo is a beginner language learning platform for French, which I began developing with zero coding background and no development team.

As its name says, FluOLinGo is built around linguistic and communicative goals, from the most basic ones to the more demanding. Each goal is represented by a numbered stop on a learning path, laying the ground for the ones that come after.

Wilkins, in 1976, called these two kinds of objective notional and functional: the language that expresses an idea, and the language that does a job. FluOLinGo keeps the pair — linguistic goals and communicative goals — and adds a third element of its own: at the end of each unit, a task that draws on both.

The main question we will examine is how a goal-based learning platform for French can be adapted for Chinese, especially since the two languages are typologically distant with contrasting functional features.

While there are shared word categories, such as verbs, adverbs, nouns, vocabulary and sentence structure, they are weighted differently in each language.

For instance, French is morphologically rich with verb conjugations that express tense, aspect and mood, while conjugation is unheard of in Chinese, which relies on aspectual markers and temporal adverbs instead.

Within the nominal phrase, definiteness and referentiality are marked in French by articles, but are determined by syntactic position and classifier in Chinese.

Then there are categories that exist in one language but not the other. Hence, French has articles and verb conjugations, while Chinese has tones and chengyu.

In Wilkins's terms (1976), FluOLinGo is neither purely synthetic nor purely analytic. Like Yalden's proportional syllabus (1983, 1987), it combines what she called structural and communicative elements, though its linguistic goals go beyond grammar to include sounds and script.

My answer is a template: a frame that remains applicable for any language.

In the rest of this presentation, we will first see how the French course is structured, down to the learner's path through one linguistic goal. Then how the template carries over to Chinese, with real examples from both textbooks. And at the end, a recipe you can take to your own language.

## 1. Two typologically distant languages

Unlike French, Chinese has no articles and no verb conjugations. What it has are measure words, verbal aspect markers, and tones.

Despite differences, there are common elements in the learning of these — and arguably all — languages. Universally relevant language learning goals include sounds, vocabulary, word forms and sentence structure, although each language weights them differently. French and Mandarin share goals in vocabulary and in sentence structure, such as the basic subject–verb–object order — the two sentences on the slide, "I go skiing" and "I drink coffee", say almost the same thing.

Romance languages tend to lean heavily on word forms, while Chinese shifts that weight toward sounds and vocabulary, and hands much of the grammatical work to sentence structure.

Mapped onto those four universal goals:

Sounds — weighted very heavily. Tones are meaning-bearing from day one: a wrong tone isn't an accent, it's a different word. The slide shows one syllable in four tones: mother, hemp, horse, scold — four distinct words. French pronunciation errors are usually forgivable; tone errors often aren't.

Vocabulary — weighted very heavily. Learners get almost no help from cognates, and the character system makes vocabulary a two-track enterprise: the spoken word and the written form must each be learned separately.

Word forms — weighted lightly. The two aspect particles on the slide stand in for tense conjugations, but they are a small closed set, not a paradigm to drill.

Sentence structure — weighted heavily. With little inflection to lean on, grammatical meaning lives in fixed word order, topic–comment patterns, and in small particles and constructions — the pair on the slide is the classic one.

Interestingly, measure words give Chinese a kind of agreement system after all: where French nouns demand the right gender for articles and adjectives, Chinese nouns demand the right classifier — the three nouns on the slide, a fish, a book and a table, each take a different one — and it largely has to be memorized along with the noun. So it's less that Chinese lacks "grammar" than that its grammar has migrated out of the word forms and into the lexicon and the sentence.

Script is the exception: some languages, including many Indigenous ones, are learnt mostly by ear, and have few or no script goals.

## 4. From 50 look-alike stops to three kinds of step

This is where FluOLinGo started. It teaches beginner French with our course textbook, L'Atelier plus A1. The first version cut the course into fifty small goals, in five units, and put them on a map: on the left, fifty stops in one line. Every exercise belonged to a goal; nothing was French in general. That is what objective-driven means here: the goal comes first. But look at three of those stops. Stop twenty-four is "to do some of an activity" — the French is on the slide: a linguistic goal. Stop twenty-nine is inviting someone and saying no, also on the slide: a communicative goal. Stop thirty is writing an email: a whole task. On the map, all three looked exactly the same, one stop each. Planning the Chinese version forced me to see that. On the right is what came out of the rethink. In each unit there are linguistic goals, the situations that use them, and one task at the end. That structure is the rest of this talk, for both languages.

## 5. One lesson page per linguistic goal

Here is the heart of it: the lesson page, which I designed myself, as a teacher, before any of the technology. Every linguistic goal gets one lesson page. When two goals are easiest to learn side by side, they share one. My original page puts "to like the" beside "to do some" — the two sentences on the slide say "I like yoga" and "I do yoga", in French. The page has six tabs, with their French names on the slide: the path tab shows where you are in the course; the concept tab explains the idea; the forms tab shows the forms; the exercise tab is where you practise; bonus is the hardest step; and the word list. The exercise tab is one sentence generator with three levels. At one star, you pick the verb form — "I like", spelled right, not the misspelling shown next to it on the slide. At two stars, you pick the verb and the article together. At three stars, you type the whole sentence. The Bonus gives you the English and asks for the French, with no help. The lesson I learned: one exercise, three levels — never separate exercises. Notice what is not there: no separate easy activity and hard activity. One page, one generator, three levels.

## 6. Each unit: linguistic goals, situations, one task

Each unit is built from two kinds of thing, and both come from the textbook. The first is linguistic goals: grammar, words and sounds — and, for Chinese, characters. Each one gets its own lesson page. The second is communicative goals, which L'Atelier calls situations. A situation is one thing you can do with the language, such as saying what you like. Where the book bundles several things into one situation, I split them, so each has one purpose. The two meet in a grid: linguistic goals down the side, situations across the top. Here is part of Unit 2. "To like the" serves saying what you like, and saying why. The negation serves several situations at once. A linguistic goal can serve many situations, and a situation can need many linguistic goals, so the grid shows me, at a glance, what a learner needs before each situation. The grid makes the combination visible: it shows which linguistic goals each situation needs. Each unit ends with one task that uses all its situations; L'Atelier calls it the Mission. In the new plan, the French course has thirty-seven situations and about sixty linguistic goals.

## 7. Saying what you like — French Unit 2

Let me walk through one situation from start to finish, so you can see how the parts fit together. French Unit 2. The situation is saying what you like — the French title is on the slide. First come the linguistic goals, each on its lesson page. The learner opens the page for "to like the", shown beside "to do some" — the two example sentences are on the slide. They work through the exercise at one star, then two, then three, and finish with the Bonus, English to French with no help. On the left is that exercise tab, from my original page. Then the situation itself, used four ways. In ÉcouTexte they hear it. In G-Compris they read a short message — on the right — and answer questions about it. In WorDrill they say it aloud. In ComposeIt they write a few lines of their own. Finally, at the end of the unit, the Mission: working out the weekend outing. It draws on every situation in the unit, not just this one. On the map, the learner sees each of these steps, one after another, in the order they do them. Nothing is a separate app; it is one path.

## 8. Frame, slots, settings

With that structure, moving to Chinese comes down to three steps. First, keep the frame. Everything not tied to one language is built once and filled twice. The frame also carries the two rules that turned out to matter most: the catalogue and the spiral. Second, change the settings. The exercises keep their shape, but what the learner must choose changes, because Chinese learners go wrong in different places. Third, fill the slots. The situations are written again, from the new language's own textbook. That is where a teacher of any language does the real work, so it gets a worked example. One step at a time.

## 9. Same loom, new thread

If you keep one picture from this part, make it a loom. The loom stays the same: that is the frame. The thread and the pattern change with every language.

## 10. The frame: built once, filled twice

First, the frame. The frame is everything that does not care which language it holds. The lesson page, one per linguistic goal. Four exercise machines: sentences, words, set phrases, and sounds. The star levels and the Bonus. The use-it activities for each situation. The unit task. And a map that shows every step of the path, in order. The last card is the newest part, settled only this week: the catalogue. Its rule is simple: never teach or test beyond what the book covers. In the plan, the website reads the catalogue in four places: a check that stops anything above the limit from being built; each lesson's path tab; a page per unit; and the search. Type the French word for "midnight" — on the slide — and it answers: Unit 2, Situation 2. Type the words for "half past" — also on the slide — and it answers: not in this course. All of it is built once and filled twice: with L'Atelier for French, and with New Practical Chinese Reader, Book 1, for Chinese. The next two slides look at how the catalogue is made, and at the rule that keeps it from becoming a cage.

## 11. Every limit has a source

How is a catalogue built? From three sources: the textbook itself, the teacher's guide, and the publisher's own unit tests. If a unit test asks for a form, that form counts as taught in that unit. Every limit points back to one of them, never to what I happen to know about French. Some examples. "Quarter past" and "half past" are not in this course: the book tells the time as "nine fifteen" and "nine thirty" — all four phrases are on the slide. "Noon" and "midnight" are in, in Unit 2. Every verb the book teaches is learned with all nine subject pronouns, from "I" to "they". And in Chinese, the book teaches the time with its own words for "a quarter", "three quarters" and "half" in lesson 11 — on the slide — so the Chinese course will teach them. Now the honest part. When I measured the first French version against the book, it reached well beyond it. Twelve colours in the first unit, where the book has two. "Why", taught four units before the book teaches it. And a pre-test that scored "half past two", a form the book never teaches. Each choice looked helpful at the time. But learners were being marked on things the course had not taught them. That is why the catalogue exists, and why, in the plan, it is checked automatically every time the site is built.

## 12. Seen · Focus · Scored

The catalogue raises a fair worry: does this mean learners never meet anything new? No. The French method I follow is a spiral. Learners meet things early, as whole phrases, and discover how they work later. So the catalogue treats three levels differently. Seen: a whole phrase that appears, but is never explained, drilled or scored. That is allowed early. Focus: explained and practised on its own lesson page. Only from the unit that teaches it. Scored: pre-tests, exercise scores, the final test. Also only from that unit. Some examples — the phrases are on the slide. "I was born in", seen long before the past tense. "How are you", long before the verb "to go". "My father, my mother, my friend", before the family and the possessives. And a second rule sits beside it: about one part in five may be new. Learners learn by struggling a little. All familiar is too easy; half unknown is too hard. The limit is that no answer may be the unknown, or depend on it. The first sentence on the slide reads "At weekends, I go … skiing." The answer is the word for "some", so it does not matter that "weekend" is new. The second reads "It is two …". The answer would be "half past", which is never taught. That one is out.

## 13. Seen · Focus · Scored, like a camera

Think of a camera. A phrase can be in the picture but out of focus: that is Seen. Then you pull it sharp: that is Focus. Only then do you take the shot: that is Scored.

## 14. The settings: the key choice moves

The settings are where the two languages really differ. The machines keep their shape, but the key choice inside them moves. In French, at two stars, the key choice is the article. You like yoga: the sentence takes "the". You do yoga: it takes "some". The two sentences are on the slide, and "the" versus "some" is exactly where a beginner goes wrong, so that is the gap the learner has to fill. Mandarin has no articles at all. So what goes in the gap? In this example, it is the verb that the object takes — all taken from the first textbook. The slide shows the three pairs: play ball, see a film, drink coffee. The learner picks which verb goes with which object. The other classic gap is the measure word. An item of clothing takes the classifier on the slide; the classic beginner error uses the all-purpose one instead. Chinese also needs two new kinds of linguistic goal that French does not have: tones, which go to the sound machine, and characters.

## 15. Same gap, different key

So the exercise keeps the gap, and the language decides which key fits. On the slide, in French, "some" fits and "the" bounces out. In Chinese, the garment classifier fits, and the all-purpose one bounces out.

## 16. A word never taught, asked once

A second idea moves across unchanged, and I think it is useful for any language. I call it the transfer test. At the end of a run, the learner is asked, once, about a word the course never taught. In French, the course teaches the two words for "a Chinese person", masculine and feminine — both on the slide. Then it asks: Luxembourgish? Nobody taught that country. But the rule was taught: a word ending in -s adds -e. So the answer follows the rule, and the slide shows it landing. The answer is not scored, because you cannot mark someone wrong for something you never told them. The feedback is the rule, not a verdict. Chinese works the same way, and the rule is even simpler: the country, plus the word for "person", gives the nationality. China gives "a Chinese person"; Britain gives "a British one" — both on the slide. Then: Luxembourg? The answer follows the same pattern, and the slide shows it. The test tells me whether the learner knows the rule, or has only memorised a list.

## 17. A key for a lock nobody showed you

The rule is a key. If the learner really holds it, it opens a lock nobody showed them: the masculine word becomes the feminine, right there on the slide.

## 18. When the book gives you nothing: NPCR Lesson 3

Here is filling the slots up close, on a real lesson: New Practical Chinese Reader, Book 1, lesson 3 — its title, "what country is she from", is on the slide. The book gives a text, notes and pronunciation work. It gives no situations and no task, and this lesson has no grammar section at all. So I start from what the text does. Its first dialogue — on the slide, line by line — asks who that is; answers, that is our teacher; asks what country she is from; answers, she is Chinese; and adds that all our teachers are Chinese. That text does two things, so it gives two situations: say who someone is, and say someone's nationality. Then the linguistic goals. The verb "to be", and its negation. The question word "who". The nationality pattern: country plus "person". The word "all" — already met in lesson 2, though the book only explains where it goes in lesson 7, so here it is used, not drilled. And a sound: the negation word changes its tone before a fourth tone, so "to be" and "not to be" are said with different tones — marked on the slide. The grid is the same grid as in French. Only the content is new, and every item in it comes from the book.

## 19. Filling the slots: the teacher writes them

Now step back to the whole course. Here are the two courses side by side. French follows L'Atelier: a welcome unit, then four units, each with a theme — the French titles are on the slide: who is it? what shall we do this weekend? where are we going this summer? and what are we eating this week? Chinese follows New Practical Chinese Reader, Book 1: fourteen lessons, grouped into five units of the same shape — lessons 1 and 2, then 3 to 6, 7 to 9, 10 and 11, and 12 to 14. The French book names its situations, adds short workshops built on a real document — the expression workshops — and ends each unit with a Mission. I mostly split and sorted what was there. The Chinese book has none of that, so every Chinese situation is drafted by the teacher, the way you just saw: twenty-six in all. Where the book has a review lesson at the end of a unit, lesson 6 and lesson 14, it becomes the unit task; for the units without one, the teacher writes the task, as with the situations. That is how the slots are filled: the same frame, the teacher's own situations, and the book still setting the limit.

## 20. Bonus material and teacher additions

Two more labels make the catalogue livable for a real teacher. The first is bonus material: things beyond the book that are still useful. They are labelled bonus on screen, never scored, never in a pre-test or the final test, and never needed to finish a step. One example: the book has almost no school subjects, but my students want to introduce themselves — "I study physics", in French on the slide. So school subjects stay, as bonus. Another: the book teaches "my", "your", "his" and "your" now, but "our" and "their" only much later. They stay visible, as bonus, and are not scored. The second label is the teacher addition: something I always teach, even though the book does not. It is taught and scored like book content. My first one is "pleased to meet you" — on the slide. The book never teaches it, and I teach it in the very first lesson. The catalogue marks each one — bonus, or teacher addition — so the automatic check accepts it, and nobody mistakes a deliberate choice for over-teaching.

## 23. AI builds fast. The teacher stays the author.

A word about how this was built, because the title promises an AI platform and no development team. I built FluOLinGo with AI coding tools. They are fast, and they made this possible for someone who cannot code. But they can misunderstand the teaching. A real example: remember the lesson page, one exercise with three levels. Earlier AI helpers split that into many separate activities. Each looked fine on its own, but the teaching idea was lost, and only a teacher could see it. Two habits fixed this. First, write every teaching decision down, in plain words, where the AI reads it every time. Second, check the real screen, not the AI's description of it. And this is where the catalogue earns its keep. The catalogue and the written rules are what keep the AI inside the teacher's decisions. When every limit has a source, and every rule is on paper, an AI helper's work can be checked against them automatically — and so can mine. The teacher must stay the author.

## 24. A recipe for your language

So here is the recipe, for a teacher of any less commonly taught or Indigenous language. One: take your textbook's two sides — the situations, what learners do, and the linguistic goals, what they learn. Two: catalogue what your book covers, and never teach or test beyond it. Every limit should point to the book, not to what you happen to know about the language. Learners may still meet a little more — I aim for about one part in five to be new — as long as the answer to a question never depends on it. Three: build one lesson page per linguistic goal, and a few exercise machines that you fill many times. Four: write your own situations where your book has none. No tool can do that part for you. Five: one exercise, more levels — never more activities. When something is too easy or too hard, add a level, not a new screen. Six: write every decision down, and check the screen. Whether you work with AI or with a person, the decisions are yours, and the screen is the proof they were followed. French to Chinese tests these steps on a hard case. If they hold there, they are worth trying for your language.
