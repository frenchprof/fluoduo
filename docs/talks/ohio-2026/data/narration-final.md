# Opening

## 1. Self-intro

Before we start, a quick word. The person you see in this video is an AI clone of me. The ideas and the words, though, are all mine.

I'm Daniel Chan. I teach at the Centre for Language Studies at NUS, and I've been teaching French for over twenty-five years.

## 2. Intro

Today I want to talk about FluOLinGo. That's capital F, O, L and G, and it stands for Fluency On Linguistic Goals. It's a platform for beginners learning French, and I built it myself, with no coding background and no development team.

## 3. What I mean by goals

As the name suggests, the whole thing is organised around goals. I use goals as a learner-facing term for what syllabus designers call learning objectives. The communicative ones are close to what ACTFL calls can-do statements.

## 4. Two kinds of goals

In the 1970s, the applied linguist David Wilkins argued that a syllabus should cover not just grammar, but also the meanings and functions learners need to express. FluOLinGo uses both. Linguistic goals are about the language itself. Communicative goals are about what you do with it, like saying what you like or inviting a friend out.

Later frameworks, like the CEFR, broke the linguistic side down further, into sounds, vocabulary, grammar and the writing system. In this talk, I split grammar into two: word forms, like verb endings, and sentence structure, like word order. So the linguistic goals come in five types: sounds, vocabulary, word forms, sentence structure and script.

## 5. The question and the plan

Wilkins was working on a framework for European languages. But could the same principles work for a language as different as Chinese?

That's the question I want to look at today. Can a goal-based platform built for French be adapted for Chinese, a language that is typologically very different? And if so, what can stay the same, and what has to change?

I'll show you how the French course works, what makes Chinese different, how the template transfers, with examples from both textbooks, and finally, a recipe you can try with your own language.

# Part 1. The French course

## 6. From fifty goals that all looked alike to three kinds of step

This is where FluOLinGo began. It teaches beginner French using our course book, L'Atelier plus A1. In the first version, I cut the course into fifty small goals across five units and laid them out on a map. That's the left side of the slide: fifty numbered goals in a row. Every exercise belonged to a goal. That's what I mean by objective-driven: the goal comes first.

But look at three of those goals. Number twenty-four, "to do some of an activity," is linguistic. Number twenty-nine, inviting someone and turning down an invitation, is communicative. Number thirty, writing an email, is a whole task.

On the map, they all looked the same. It was planning the Chinese version that made me notice this. On the right is the revised structure. Each unit now has three kinds of step: linguistic goals, the situations that use them, and one task at the end. Only the first two are goals. That structure runs through the rest of this talk, for both languages.

## 7. One lesson page per linguistic goal

This is the core of it: the lesson page. I designed it myself, as a teacher, before any of the tech came in. Every linguistic goal gets its own page. If two goals are easier to learn side by side, they share a page.

On my original page, "to like the" sits next to "to do some." The two sentences on the slide say "I like yoga" and "I do yoga" in French.

The six tabs take learners from the course path through the concept, forms and exercises, then on to the Bonus and word list.

The exercise tab is a single sentence generator with three levels. At one star, you pick the verb form: "I like," spelled correctly. You can see the common misspelling next to it on the slide. At two stars, you pick the verb and the article together. At three stars, you type the whole sentence yourself. Then the Bonus gives you the English and asks for the French, with no help at all.

What I learned here: one exercise, three levels. Keep it on one page, with one generator. That's it.

## 8. Each unit: linguistic goals, situations, one task

Each unit combines linguistic goals and communicative goals, which L'Atelier calls situations. Each linguistic goal gets a lesson page, while each situation represents something learners can do with the language. Sometimes the book packs several things into one situation. When it does, I split them up, so each situation has one purpose.

The two kinds meet in a grid. Linguistic goals go down the side, situations go across the top. Here's part of Unit 2. "To like the" helps you say what you like, and also say why. Negation turns up in several situations at once. One linguistic goal can serve lots of situations, and one situation can need lots of linguistic goals. So with the grid, I can see at a glance what a learner needs before they tackle each situation.

In Wilkins's terms, this makes FluOLinGo a bit synthetic, building language up piece by piece, and a bit analytic, starting from what learners need to do with it. It's close to what Janice Yalden called a proportional syllabus, in that it combines structure and communication.

Every unit finishes with one task that uses all its situations. L'Atelier calls it the Mission. In the new plan, the French course has thirty-seven situations and around sixty linguistic goals.

# Part 2. Two languages that are far apart

## 11. Where French and Chinese put the weight

Chinese has no articles and no verb conjugations. What it does have is measure words, aspect markers, and tones.

Still, the same linguistic goal types apply to both languages, and I'd say to any language. Every language weights them differently. French and Mandarin overlap on vocabulary and sentence structure. Both use subject, verb, object. Look at the two sentences on the slide, "I go skiing" and "I drink coffee." They're built almost the same way.

Romance languages put a lot of weight on word forms. Chinese moves that weight over to sounds and vocabulary, and lets sentence structure do most of the grammar.

So let's go through the first four.

Sounds carry a huge amount of weight. Tones matter from the very first day. Get the tone wrong and you've said a different word. On the slide you can see one syllable in four tones: mother, hemp, horse, scold. Four separate words. In French, a pronunciation mistake usually gets forgiven. In Chinese, a tone mistake often won't.

Vocabulary is also very heavy. There are almost no cognates to help you. And because of the characters, every word has to be learned twice: once by ear and once in writing.

Word forms are light. Aspect particles do some of the work that tense endings do in French, without the conjugation tables.

Sentence structure is heavy. With little inflection, grammar relies on word order, topic and comment, and particles. The pair on the slide is the classic example.

Here's something interesting. Measure words give Chinese its own kind of agreement. In French, every noun has a gender, and the article and adjective have to match it. In Chinese, every noun has its own classifier. On the slide you've got a fish, a book and a table, and each takes a different one. You basically have to learn the classifier along with the noun. So Chinese has plenty of grammar. It's just moved out of the word endings and into the vocabulary and the sentence.

Script is the odd one out. For Chinese, it's a whole track of its own. But some languages, including many Indigenous languages, are learned mostly by listening, so they have few script goals, or none.

# Part 3. Moving the template to Chinese

## 12. Frame, slots, settings

Moving to Chinese takes three steps.

Step one: keep the frame. Build the language-independent parts once. The frame also holds two important rules: the catalogue and the spiral.

Step two: change the settings. The exercises keep their shape, but what learners have to choose changes.

Step three: fill the slots. Write situations from the new language's textbook. That's where the teacher does the real work.

Think of a loom. The loom stays the same. That's the frame. The thread and pattern change with each language.

Let's take the steps one at a time.

## 13. The frame: built once, filled twice

The frame is everything that works the same whatever language you put in it.

It includes the lesson pages, four exercise machines for sentences, words, set phrases and sounds, star levels, Bonus, situation activities, unit tasks and learning map. For Chinese, the activities keep their structure, but the texts and words change.

The last card is the newest piece. I only settled it this week. It's the catalogue. The rule is simple: never teach or test anything the book doesn't cover.

In the plan, the site checks the catalogue in four places. There's a check that blocks anything beyond the limit from being built. There's the path tab on each lesson. There's a page for each unit. And there's the search. Type the French word for "midnight," the one on the slide, and it tells you: Unit 2, Situation 2. Type "half past," and it tells you: that's not in this course.

All of this is built once and filled twice. Once with L'Atelier for French, and once with New Practical Chinese Reader, Book 1, for Chinese.

The next two slides show how the catalogue gets made, and the rule that stops it from turning into a cage.

## 14. Every limit has a source

How do you build a catalogue? From three sources: the textbook, the teacher's guide, and the publisher's unit tests. If a unit test asks for something, it counts as taught in that unit. Every limit goes back to one of those sources. My own knowledge of French doesn't count.

Some examples. "Quarter past" and "half past" aren't in this course. The book tells the time as "nine fifteen" and "nine thirty." "Noon" and "midnight" are in, in Unit 2. Every verb the book teaches gets learned with all nine subject pronouns. And in Chinese, lesson 11 teaches the time with its own words for "a quarter," "three quarters" and "half." So the Chinese course will teach them.

Now, I have to be honest here. When I checked the first French version against the book, it went way past it. Twelve colours in the first unit, when the book has two. "Why" taught four units before the book gets to it. And a pre-test that marked learners on "half past two," which the book never teaches.

Every one of those choices seemed helpful at the time. But learners were being marked on things the course hadn't taught them. That's why the catalogue exists, and why the plan calls for automatic checks every time the site is built.

## 15. Seen, Focus, Scored

Now, you might be thinking: so learners never come across anything new? They do. The French approach I follow is a spiral. Learners meet things early, as whole phrases, and figure out how they work later.

So the catalogue treats things at three levels.

Seen means a whole phrase shows up, but it's never explained, drilled or marked.

Focus means it's explained and practised on its own lesson page, from the unit that teaches it.

Scored means it counts: pre-tests, exercise scores, the final test. Again, only from the unit that teaches it.

Think of a camera. Something can be in the shot but blurry. That's Seen. Then you bring it into focus. That's Focus. And only then do you take the picture. That's Scored.

For example, learners meet "I was born in" before studying the past tense, and "How are you?" before learning the verb "to go."

There's a second rule. About one part in five can be new. People learn by struggling a bit. If everything's familiar, it's too easy. If half of it is unknown, it's too hard. But the answer can never be the new thing, or depend on it.

Look at the first sentence on the slide: "At weekends, I go … skiing." The answer is the word for "some," so it doesn't matter that "weekend" is new. The second one says "It is two …" There the answer would be "half past," which is never taught. So that one's out.

## 16. The settings: the key choice moves

The settings are where the two languages really part ways. The machines keep their shape, but the key choice inside them shifts.

In French, at two stars, the key choice is the article. You like yoga, so it takes "the." You do yoga, so it takes "some." The two sentences are on the slide. "The" versus "some" is exactly where beginners go wrong, so that's the gap the learner has to fill.

Mandarin has no articles. So what goes in the gap? In this example, it's the verb that goes with the object. These all come from the first textbook. The slide shows three pairs: play ball, see a film, drink coffee. The learner matches each verb to its object.

The other classic gap is the measure word. A piece of clothing takes the classifier on the slide. The typical beginner mistake is to use the general-purpose one.

So the exercise keeps its gap, and the language decides which key fits. In French, "some" fits and "the" bounces off. In Chinese, the classifier for clothing fits, and the general one bounces off.

Chinese also adds new goals within two of the types: tones, which go into the sound machine, and characters.

## 17. A word never taught, asked once

There's a second idea that carries over unchanged, and I think it's useful for any language. I call it the transfer test.

At the end of a run, the learner gets asked one question about a word the course never taught.

In French, the course teaches the masculine and feminine words for "a Chinese person." Then it asks: what about Luxembourgish? Nobody taught that one. But learners know the rule: if the word ends in -s, you add -e. So the answer follows from the rule, and you can see it land on the slide.

That answer isn't marked. You can't mark someone wrong on something you never told them. The feedback simply gives them the rule.

Chinese works the same way, and the rule's even simpler: country plus the word for "person" gives you the nationality. China gives you "a Chinese person." Britain gives you "a British person." Then: Luxembourg? Same pattern, and the slide shows the answer.

What this tells me is whether the learner has actually got the rule, or just memorised a list.

## 18. Filling the slots: the teacher writes them

Now the third step. Here are the two courses side by side.

French follows L'Atelier. A welcome unit, then four themed units. Their French titles are on the slide.

Chinese follows New Practical Chinese Reader, Book 1, with its fourteen lessons regrouped into five units.

The French book names its situations, adds short workshops built around real documents, which it calls expression workshops, and ends each unit with a Mission. For French, I mostly split up and sorted what was already there.

The Chinese book has none of that. So every Chinese situation gets drafted by the teacher. Twenty-six of them in total. Where the book has a review lesson at the end of a unit, in lessons 6 and 14, that becomes the unit task. For the other units, the teacher writes the task too.

Let me show you what that drafting looks like.

## 19. When the book gives you nothing: NPCR Lesson 3

New Practical Chinese Reader, Book 1, lesson 3. Its title, "What country is she from?", is on the slide.

The book gives you a text, some notes, and pronunciation practice. No situations, no task, and this lesson doesn't even have a grammar section.

So I start from what the text actually does. The first dialogue is on the slide, line by line. Someone asks who that is. The answer: that's our teacher. Then: what country is she from? She's Chinese. And then: all our teachers are Chinese.

That gives me two situations: saying who someone is, and saying someone's nationality.

Next, the linguistic goals. The verb "to be," and how to make it negative. The question word "who." The nationality pattern: country plus "person." The word "all." Learners already met it in lesson 2, but the book only explains where it goes in lesson 7, so here they use it without drilling it. And one sound: the negative word changes its tone before a fourth tone, so "is" and "is not" are said with different tones. That's marked on the slide.

It's the same grid as in French. Only the content is new, and everything comes from the book.

The architecture stays the same. The linguistic goals, exercise settings and situations are adapted to Chinese. And the textbook determines what learners are expected to know. That's how the French template becomes a Chinese one.

## 20. Bonus material and teacher additions

There are two more labels that make the catalogue workable for a real teacher.

The first is bonus material: things beyond the book that are still useful. They're labelled "bonus" on screen, never marked, and never needed to finish a step.

For example, the book has almost no school subjects, but my students want to introduce themselves. "I study physics," in French on the slide. So school subjects stay in, as bonus.

The second label is the teacher addition. That's something I always teach, even if the book doesn't. It's taught and marked just like book content. My first one is "pleased to meet you," there on the slide. The book never teaches it, and I teach it in the very first lesson.

The catalogue tags each as bonus or teacher addition. That way the automatic check lets them through, and nobody mistakes a deliberate choice for over-teaching.

# Part 4. Closing

## 21. AI builds fast. The teacher stays the author.

A word about how I built this, since the title talks about an AI platform with no development team.

I built FluOLinGo with AI coding tools. They're fast, and they made it possible for someone like me, who can't code. But they can get the teaching wrong.

Here's a real example. Remember the lesson page, with one exercise and three levels? Earlier AI helpers broke that up into lots of separate activities. Each looked fine by itself, but the teaching idea behind it was gone, and only a teacher would notice.

Two habits fixed that. First, write down every teaching decision in plain words, somewhere the AI reads it every time. Second, look at the actual screen yourself, because the AI's description of it can be wrong.

The catalogue and written rules keep the AI inside the teacher's decisions. When every limit has a source, you can check an AI helper's work against it automatically. And you can check mine the same way.

The teacher has to stay the author.

## 22. A recipe for your language

So here's the recipe, for anyone teaching a less commonly taught language or an Indigenous language.

One. Take the two sides of your textbook: the situations, which are what learners do, and the linguistic goals, which are what they learn.

Two. Catalogue what your book covers. Keep teaching and assessment within those limits, while allowing controlled exposure to new language.

Three. Build one lesson page per linguistic goal, and a few exercise machines you can fill again and again.

Four. Write your own situations where your book doesn't give you any. No tool can do that part for you.

Five. One exercise, more levels. If something's too easy or too hard, add a level and keep the same screen.

Six. Write down your decisions and check the actual screens. Whether you're working with AI or with a person, the teacher remains the author.

French to Chinese is a tough test for these steps. If they hold up there, they're worth trying with your language.
